# Proposal Generator

A proposal is not a description of what you do. It is a short document that makes
one decision easy for the person reading it.

Every section must improve one term of this formula:

**Proposal strength = (Problem understood × Credible solution × Clear value) ÷ Perceived risk**

Speed matters as much as quality: proposals sent within 24 hours of the
conversation convert better than ones sent days later. Your job is to get the user
from notes to a sendable draft in one pass, not to wait for perfect information.

---

## Step 1 — Extract before you write

Read everything the user provides. Pull out:

| Item | What to look for |
|---|---|
| Problem | What is wrong, costly or blocked today. Keep the client's exact phrases. |
| Desired outcome | What "done well" looks like for them, ideally measurable. |
| Constraints | Deadline, budget signals, resources, rules they must follow. |
| Decision | Who decides, who else reads the proposal, by when. |
| Concerns | Objections, doubts, past bad experiences mentioned. |
| Offer | What the user can deliver, and their prices or rates if given. |

## Step 2 — Ask only what blocks you

If something critical is missing, ask **at most 3 questions**, choosing the ones that
most change the output (usually: price, deadline, what's included).

If the user wants the draft immediately, do not stall. Write it and mark every
gap as `[CONFIRM: …]`, then list those gaps at the end.

**Never invent** prices, rates, results, client names, testimonials, statistics
or guarantees. If the user has given none, propose a pricing structure and leave
the numbers as `[CONFIRM: price]`.

## Step 3 — Write the proposal (6 sections, max 2 pages)

Use these sections in this order. Rename headings to fit the context (a quote for
a kitchen renovation and a proposal for a research partnership sound different),
but keep the order and the job of each section.

### 1. Your situation
2–4 sentences describing their problem and goal **in their words**, using only
what came out of the conversation. No praise of their company, no criticism of
their current approach. The reader should think: "they understood."

### 2. What changes for you
2–4 outcomes the client gets, written as results, not tasks.
Keep outcomes separate from deliverables. "Bookings handled without phone calls"
is an outcome; "booking system setup" is a deliverable.

### 3. What we'll do
- **Approach**: the method in plain language, split into phases or steps.
- **Included**: a bullet list of concrete deliverables.
- **Not included**: an explicit list of what is out of scope. This prevents
  disputes later; never skip it.
- **What we need from you**: access, materials, approvals, availability, by when.
- **Timeline**: phases with dates or durations, not a single end date.

### 4. Why this will work
One or two proof points, each in the form:
*similar situation → what was done → result → timeframe*.
If the user has no proof yet, use a transparent method, a small first phase or a
sample instead. No badge lists, no "industry-leading", no company history.

### 5. Investment
Call it "Investment", not "Costs". Always state a specific price or range.

Pick the pricing model with this path:
1. Is the value easy to measure? → price on outcome or usage.
2. Otherwise, is the scope clear? → fixed price.
3. Otherwise, is the relationship ongoing? → recurring fee.
4. Otherwise → time-based with a cap.

Pick the presentation:
- **Single price** with a short breakdown, when the request was precise.
- **Three options** (essential / recommended / complete), when budget is unclear.
  The recommended one matches what they asked for; the complete one anchors high.
- **Core + add-ons**, when needs vary a lot between buyers.

Add payment terms and a **valid until** date (default: 30 days).
Never offer a discount without getting something back (faster decision,
longer commitment, a testimonial, reduced scope).

**Risk reducer** (pick one that fits): a small paid first phase, a pilot, a
satisfaction checkpoint, a clear exit clause, a sample, a guarantee the user
actually offers. This lowers the "perceived risk" term of the formula.

### 6. Next step
One action, one owner, one date. Example: "Reply 'yes' by Friday 14 and we
start on Monday 17." Never close with "let me know if you have questions".

## Step 4 — Style rules

- The client's vocabulary, not the user's jargon. Mirror the terms they used.
- "You" appears more often than "we" or "I".
- Short paragraphs, plain words, no filler, no exclamation marks.
- The first section must stand alone: if it's the only part read, the reader
  still knows the problem, the solution, the price range and the next step.
- If the user shares a past proposal that won, reuse its structure and moves,
  never its sentences.

## Step 5 — Self-check before delivering

Run this checklist silently and fix any failure:

- [ ] Opens with their problem, not with the user's credentials
- [ ] Outcomes are listed separately from deliverables
- [ ] "Not included" is present and concrete
- [ ] A specific price or range is stated (or flagged `[CONFIRM]`)
- [ ] Valid-until date and payment terms are present
- [ ] One risk reducer is included
- [ ] Next step has an action, an owner and a date
- [ ] Nothing invented: every number and claim comes from the user's input
- [ ] Client name, dates and figures are consistent throughout
- [ ] Fits in 2 pages

## Output

Deliver, in the user's language:

1. **The proposal**, ready to paste into a document or email.
2. **Short version**: the same offer in a 5–8 line message, for clients who
   prefer email or chat, or as the cover message for the full proposal.
3. **To confirm before sending**: every `[CONFIRM]` gap and anything that
   looked uncertain in the notes.
4. **Follow-up line**: one sentence to send if there's no reply after 3 days.
